# What accessibility means

Web accessibility is the practice of building sites that people with disabilities can actually use. That includes people who are blind or have low vision, people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people who cannot use a mouse and rely on a keyboard or other input, people with cognitive differences, and people whose situation is temporary or situational, like a broken arm or bright sunlight. Accessibility is not a feature for a small minority; it is the difference between a site that works for everyone and one that quietly excludes a large number of people.

## The standard: WCAG and its four ideas

The common reference for web accessibility is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, usually called WCAG. It organizes its guidance under four ideas that are worth knowing as a frame. Content should be perceivable, meaning people can take it in through sight, sound, or touch, which covers things like text alternatives for images and enough contrast. The interface should be operable, meaning people can navigate and use it, including by keyboard alone. The content should be understandable, meaning it reads and behaves predictably. And the markup should be compatible with the range of browsers and assistive technologies people use, meaning it is coded in standard ways that those tools can interpret. Most practical accessibility work maps to one of these four.

## Conformance levels

WCAG defines levels of conformance, commonly referenced as A, AA, and AAA, with AA being the level most organizations and regulations target. AA is a sensible goal for most sites: it covers the things that matter most without demanding the strictest possible bar on everything. When a requirement or a baseline mentions WCAG 2.1 AA, that is what it means, a defined set of checkable success criteria at the middle level. You do not need to memorize the criteria to do good work; the basics in this reference cover most of them.

## Why it is not optional

Beyond being the right thing to do, accessibility is increasingly a legal expectation. Many jurisdictions require public-facing sites to be accessible, and accessibility complaints and lawsuits are common, especially in areas like retail, services, and anything government-related. A site that excludes people using assistive technology is both shutting out customers and taking on legal risk. The security and legal baseline in this portfolio targets WCAG 2.1 AA for exactly this reason. Treating accessibility as a basic requirement, like security, rather than an optional polish, is the right posture.

## The good news: the basics cover most of it

The reassuring part is that a large share of accessibility problems come from a small set of basic mistakes, and fixing those basics gets you most of the way. Enough color contrast, real selectable text instead of text baked into images, every control reachable and usable by keyboard, images described for people who cannot see them, a clear heading structure, and forms whose fields are properly labelled.
